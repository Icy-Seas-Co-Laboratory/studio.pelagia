<p class="pelagia-kicker">Field proven in the Northern Gulf of Alaska</p>
<p class="lead">Pelagia grew from direct experience operating and processing data from the University of Alaska Fairbanks Deep-Focus Particle Imager for the Northern Gulf of Alaska Long-Term Ecological Research program.</p>

<section class="work-section work-feature">
  <div>
    <h2>An oceanographic observatory, not simply a camera.</h2>
    <p>The Deep-Focus Particle Imager (DPI) is an actively controlled, undulating towed vehicle in the ISIIS family. The UAF/NGA configuration combines three high-resolution line-scan shadowgraph cameras with acoustic and environmental sensors. Organisms and particles are observed in place, without collection or physical handling, while the vehicle crosses depth layers and hydrographic gradients.</p>
    <p>Each image is part of a spatially and temporally indexed observation stream. Time, position, vehicle depth, camera, sampling context, environmental measurements, and processing history all matter alongside the pixels.</p>

<section class="work-section">
  <h2>A data stream measured in millions of liters and hundreds of terabytes.</h2>
  <p>The reported UAF/NGA operating configuration creates an unusually demanding image-processing problem. Exact rates vary with camera and acquisition settings, but the scale is constant: sustained shipboard acquisition, sparse targets within an immense background, and far more imagery than a science team can inspect manually.</p>
  <div class="pelagia-metrics field-metrics">
    <div class="pelagia-metric"><strong>3 cameras</strong><span>High-resolution line-scan shadowgraph imagery</span></div>
    <div class="pelagia-metric"><strong>≈240 L s⁻¹</strong><span>Water imaged by the reported UAF/NGA configuration</span></div>
    <div class="pelagia-metric"><strong>≈0.7–0.9 TB h⁻¹</strong><span>Configuration-specific imagery production</span></div>
    <div class="pelagia-metric"><strong>Continuous</strong><span>Multi-hour surveys through the water column</span></div>

</section>

<section class="work-section">
  <p class="pelagia-kicker">Instrument to data</p>
  <h2>One traceable operational workflow.</h2>
  <div class="instrument-workflow" aria-label="DPI processing workflow">
    <span>Tow and acquire</span><span>Preserve raw imagery</span><span>Synchronize sensors</span><span>Segment candidates</span><span>Classify</span><span>Review</span><span>Aggregate and export</span>

  <p>Pelagia reflects the full lifecycle: reliable acquisition, high-throughput storage and transfer, distributed processing, automated detection and classification, scientific review, and analysis-ready export. Every derived region, mask, measurement, and classification can remain traceable to its source imagery, acquisition metadata, processing configuration, and review state.</p>
</section>

<section class="work-section">
  <h2>Fine-scale imagery changes what scientists can see.</h2>
  <p>Traditional plankton nets remain essential, but they can miss fine spatial structure and damage fragile gelatinous organisms. DPI observations preserve organisms in their oceanographic setting. Research from the 2022–2023 NGA deployments found aggregations of ctenophores, hydromedusae, and siphonophores around frontal features, reported previously undetected species records, and showed that gelatinous zooplankton were more prominent in the ecosystem than historical sampling had indicated.</p>
  <div class="work-card-grid">
    <div class="pelagia-card"><h3>Biological distributions</h3><p>Map abundance, size, and fragile gelatinous zooplankton across depth and distance.</p></div>
    <div class="pelagia-card"><h3>Environmental context</h3><p>Relate biological patches to temperature, salinity, oxygen, acoustics, and frontal structure.</p></div>
    <div class="pelagia-card"><h3>Defensible records</h3><p>Create quality-controlled, georeferenced observations for ecological analysis and model training.</p></div>
  </div>
</section>
